The Trilogy

The American Supplement Investigation

Why do millions of Americans spend billions of dollars on supplements whose benefits may be uncertain, whose claims can exceed the evidence, and whose risks are often poorly understood? A three-part investigation follows the evidence from the medicine cabinet to a recommendation for what should change.

What the Label Doesn't Tell You

Evidence, anecdote, and the vitamin that reversed a generation of advice

A doctor recommended it. The biology made sense. Then the trial found the opposite. Built around the beta-carotene lung cancer trials of the 1990s, this volume teaches readers to tell a mechanism from a result, and a testimonial from proof — the toolkit for everything that follows.

The Hollow Aisle

Why This Investigation

Why "The American Supplement Investigation"?

Nearly three in four American adults take a dietary supplement. Most assume — reasonably, given how similar the packaging looks to regulated medicine — that some level of government testing stands behind the claim on the label. In most cases, none does.

This is not an argument that supplements are fraudulent. Some work, for some people, for some purposes, and the evidence for a few is genuinely strong. It is an argument that the confidence with which a product is chosen and the evidence that actually exists for it are, far too often, two very different things.

"I write as an investigator, not an advocate. The most dangerous claims are rarely the obviously false ones. They're the ones that are technically true, biologically plausible, and still misleading."

Drawing on Robert Coleman's forty years in medicine — sixteen as Director of Clinical Pharmacy Services at the VA Palo Alto Health Care System — and the clinical pharmacy expertise of co-authors Joy Meier and Julio Lopez, this investigation applies the same evidence-based standard as Broken Promises Healthcare to a $60 billion industry most Americans engage with every single day.

How We Source Claims

Every Figure, Sourced

Every factual claim in this investigation is sourced — to peer-reviewed research, federal regulatory documents, or government data. Where a figure is an estimate or a range, we say so. If you believe a citation has been misrepresented, tell us — we want to know.
