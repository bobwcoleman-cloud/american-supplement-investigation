Frequently Asked Questions

In a U.S. Pharmacopeia survey of practicing pharmacists, the three most common questions consumers ask about a supplement were: "Is this right for me?", "Will it interact with my other medications?", and "Which product should I use?" These, and the questions people most often search for online, answered honestly and sourced — each linking to the fuller chapter or guide behind it.

Safety & Interactions

Will this supplement interact with my prescription medications? Possibly, and it's one of the most under-checked risks in medicine today. Documented interactions include St. John's Wort reducing the effectiveness of immunosuppressants and chemotherapy, fish oil and vitamin E compounding anticoagulant bleeding risk, and chromium or cinnamon compounding a diabetes medication's blood-sugar-lowering effect. Most interaction-checking software was built around prescription drugs and doesn't reliably cover supplements. See The Hollow Aisle, Chapter 7.

Is it safe to take supplements during pregnancy? This varies enormously by supplement and deserves a direct conversation with an OB-GYN or pharmacist rather than a general answer — some, like folate, are actively recommended; others, like high-dose vitamin A or certain herbs (ashwagandha among them), carry documented risk. This is not a category where "natural" implies "safe" — see the next question.

Does "natural" mean it's safe? No. Foxglove is entirely natural and is also the source of a compound powerful enough to stop a heart at the wrong dose. Ephedra was natural and linked to over 150 deaths before its 2004 ban. Naturalness describes where a compound came from, not what it does inside your body. See When Natural Isn't Safe.

What happens if I take too much of a vitamin or mineral? It depends on the nutrient. Water-soluble vitamins (C, the B-complex) are mostly excreted and rarely reach dangerous levels. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) accumulate in the body and can reach genuinely toxic levels with sustained high-dose use. Iron overdose is a leading cause of fatal accidental poisoning in young children. More is not simply better for any nutrient on this list.

Can a supplement be contaminated or not contain what the label says? Yes, and it's documented more often than most people assume. A national liver injury registry found 40% of green tea extract products implicated in liver injury didn't even list the ingredient on their label. FDA's public warning database tracks products — mostly in weight-loss and sexual-enhancement categories — found spiked with undisclosed pharmaceutical ingredients. See The Hollow Aisle, Chapter 4.

Is there lead in my protein powder? Often, in trace amounts, and it varies widely by product and lot. Consumer Reports tested 23 products in 2025 and found more than two-thirds exceeded its own conservative benchmark of 0.5 micrograms of lead per serving, but only two exceeded 5 micrograms, the limit in USP's supplement standard. FDA has set no limit specific to protein powder. Independent results are mixed: plant-based powders averaged more lead than whey in CR's and the Clean Label Project's tests, but a peer-reviewed European study of 90 products found no significant difference — so a brand's own published test results tell you more than its protein source. There's no reason to panic, but daily users, pregnant women, and parents of young children should be pickier. See the protein powder entry.

Evidence & Efficacy

Does this supplement actually work? The honest answer requires asking a follow-up question: works for what, and compared to what evidence? A product can have a genuine, narrow effect while the marketing implies something far broader — omega-3 shows a real reduction in heart attack risk but not the stroke or overall cardiovascular benefit implied by "heart health" claims. See How to Read a Health Claim.

What does "clinically studied" or "clinically proven" actually mean? Nothing specific and legally enforceable. The phrase can describe a single small, unpublished, industry-funded study on a dozen people, or a large independent randomized trial — the label doesn't have to tell you which.

If a friend/family member says it worked for them, isn't that good evidence? It's a real experience, but not proof the supplement caused it. Four ordinary explanations can produce the same story: confirmation bias, the placebo effect, regression to the mean, and the natural fluctuation of symptoms. See What the Label Doesn't Tell You, Chapter 3.

Can a supplement have a scientific-sounding mechanism and still not work? Yes — this is one of the best-documented patterns in supplement research. Beta-carotene's antioxidant mechanism was biologically sound and its cancer-prevention trials still found the opposite of what was predicted, increasing lung cancer risk in smokers. A plausible mechanism tells you an effect is possible, not that it happens.

Why do some supplements only work for certain people? Because the underlying evidence is often conditional on a real deficiency. Vitamin D, B12, calcium, and iron are the clearest examples: genuinely effective if you're actually deficient, with little demonstrated benefit for people who already have adequate levels. See the Top 25 Supplements guide for the full breakdown.

Regulation & Trust

Are supplements approved by the FDA? No — and this surprises most people. A dietary supplement can reach a U.S. shelf with no premarket review of its safety or effectiveness. The FDA's role is almost entirely reactive, responding to adverse events after a product is already on the market. See The Regulatory Gap.

What does "third-party tested" mean, and can I trust it? It depends entirely on who did the testing. A named, independent certifier (NSF International, USP, ConsumerLab) applies real, disclosed standards. An unnamed internal lab with no public standard can also technically satisfy the phrase "third-party tested." Look for the certifier's name specifically.

Why is a supplement allowed to say "supports heart health" without proving it treats heart disease? Because U.S. law draws a sharp line between a "structure/function claim" (requires no clinical evidence, only a disclaimer) and a "disease claim" (would make it an unapproved drug). Most supplement marketing is written to sit as close as possible to that line without crossing it. See The Hollow Aisle, Chapter 3.

Do other countries regulate supplements more strictly? Yes. Canada requires a product license before sale. Australia sorts products into three risk tiers with evidence requirements scaled to the claim. The EU only permits a pre-approved list of ingredients at all. The U.S. is the outlier, not the norm. See What Other Countries Do.

Who actually profits when I buy a supplement, and does it depend on whether it works? Subscription models, affiliate commissions (typically 10–50% of a sale), and multi-level marketing structures all generate revenue independent of whether the product benefits the buyer. See Who Profits From Belief.

Are there legal limits for heavy metals in supplements? Not as a federal numeric limit, for most supplements in the U.S. FDA requires good manufacturing practices that include guarding against contaminants, but leaves manufacturers to set their own specifications. It has set interim reference levels for dietary lead (2.2 micrograms a day for children, 8.8 for women of childbearing age; see its Closer to Zero program) but none for protein powders. USP's voluntary standard applies only to products that claim it, and California's Proposition 65 requires a warning label rather than banning anything. The EU sets binding maximums for lead and cadmium in food supplements. As of September 2026, Texas's attorney general is investigating protein powder makers, and a California testing-and-disclosure bill did not advance out of the Senate. See The Regulatory Gap.

Practical Dosing

How much of a vitamin or mineral do I actually need? Almost always less than what high-dose products market. Recommended intakes are set to prevent deficiency, not to maximize a benefit that increases indefinitely with dose — for most nutrients, benefit plateaus well below the doses sold as "extra strength."

Can I take multiple supplements that contain the same ingredient without realizing it? Very easily, and it's one of the most common ways someone unintentionally reaches an excessive dose — a multivitamin, a separate vitamin D pill, and a fortified greens powder can each contribute to the same nutrient without anyone adding up the total.

Is the dose on the label the dose actually in the product? Not reliably for every category. Independent testing of melatonin products, for example, has repeatedly found actual content varying from the labeled dose by wide margins. This is a documented quality-control gap, not a rare exception.

Should I tell my doctor about every supplement I take, even "harmless" ones like vitamins? Yes, specifically because most people don't. Physicians are inconsistently trained to ask about supplements, and patients rarely volunteer them unprompted because they don't mentally file a vitamin under "medication." Bring a full list, or use a printable reconciliation worksheet (PDF), to every appointment.

Is It Worth It?

Do I need a multivitamin if I already eat a reasonably varied diet? The trial evidence is weaker than the "insurance policy" framing suggests. Large studies (COSMOS-Mind, Physicians' Health Study II) have found null-to-modest effects in well-nourished adults — the single largest supplement category by sales has some of the thinnest evidence for its broadest claim.

Is the expensive brand actually better than the cheap one? Not necessarily, and price alone tells you nothing about evidence quality, purity, or dose accuracy. A genuine third-party certification (see above) is a better signal than price.

Which supplements have the strongest evidence behind them? The best-evidenced entries share a common trait: they make a narrow, specific, testable claim rather than a broad promise. Creatine for short-duration athletic performance and protein powder for muscle recovery are two of the clearest examples. (That evidence concerns the effect; a given product's purity is a separate question — see the lead question above.) See the full Top 25 ranking.

Do I need protein powder? Most healthy adults don't. The recommended dietary allowance is about 0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight (roughly 0.36 grams per pound), which works out to about 61 grams a day for a 170-pound adult. Consumer Reports, citing the latest federal survey of U.S. eating habits, reports that Americans of every age group already exceed that recommendation. Some groups do benefit from more — older adults, pregnant women, and serious athletes — and a powder can be a convenient way to get it, but whole foods can do the same job without the contamination question (see above). The evidence for protein is about its effect; it isn't an argument for buying a powder. See the protein powder entry.

If the evidence is this mixed, should I stop taking supplements altogether? Not necessarily — that's not this investigation's argument. Several products on this site's own ranking have genuine, replicated benefit for specific people. The goal isn't to empty the medicine cabinet; it's to know which claims in it have actually been tested, and which haven't.
